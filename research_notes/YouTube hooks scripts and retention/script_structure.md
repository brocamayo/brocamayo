## 1. Script frameworks taught by top creators and strategists (with a template for each)

### Takeaway
The frameworks with the best support all say the same thing in different words: each section should be **caused by** the last one and should **create unresolved tension** that the next section pays off ("but/therefore", setup→tension→payoff, open loops). Veritasium's "misconception first" approach is the one framework here with a real learning-science basis (Muller's PhD research). Per-creator specifics for Paddy Galloway, Film Booth ("content–context"), and Johnny Harris could not be verified from primary sources in this pass.

### Cited Findings

**"But / Therefore" (Trey Parker and Matt Stone, South Park)**
- Parker and Stone's rule is to link scenes by cause and effect with "but" or "therefore" instead of "and then", so each episode has "a sense of cause and effect, tension and rising action." Parker calls it "The Rule Of Replacing 'Ands' With Either 'Buts' Or 'Therefores'" (from the documentary *6 Days to Air* and an NYU guest lecture clip; the wording comes from secondary transcriptions, not a primary transcript) — [VeryGoodCopy](https://verygoodcopy.com/verygoodcopy-blog-4/south-park-copywriting); [Script Magazine](https://scriptmag.com/screenplays/twitter-screenwriting-tips-story-outline-essentials-therefore-but-because)
- David Perell's write-up shows a worked example: a flat "and then" account of a mall trip turned into a chain where each beat causes or complicates the next (the car is gone, so the day gets worse) — [Perell](https://perell.com/note/but-therefore-rule/)
- Commentators carry the rule beyond fiction to talks and pitches: "too many 'and thens' and you have lost your audience" — [Impactful Speaking](https://impactfulspeaking.substack.com/p/why-some-stories-are-boring-and-how)

**Setup → Tension → Payoff loop (George Blackman, former scriptwriter for Ali Abdaal)**
- Blackman's framework works **per segment**, not over the whole video. It has three beats, setup, tension and payoff, and he also uses it to read retention graphs — [VidPros summary](https://vidpros.com/insiders/george-blackman-youtube-scriptwriting/) (search snippet only; the page fetch failed)
- He weighs giving information early, to keep interest, against saving the ultimate payoff for the end, and builds the script toward the final payoff — [Chaindesk summary of Blackman interview](https://chaindesk.ai/tools/youtube-summarizer/the-you-tube-scriptwriter-getting-millions-of-views-5xvHonICNl8) (AI summary)
- A common mistake he names is never stating the **main question** the video will answer. His fix is to break talking points down and find the most important ones — [Chaindesk summary](https://chaindesk.ai/tools/youtube-summarizer/the-you-tube-scriptwriter-getting-millions-of-views-5xvHonICNl8)
- A comparison guide credits "hook-build-payoff loops" to Ben Kitchen and George Blackman: "every section opens a loop and closes it before opening the next" (paraphrase) — [OutlierKit methods compared](https://outlierkit.com/resources/youtube-scriptwriting-methods-compared/) (search snippet)
- Blackman also appears on Creator Science (Jay Clouse) — [Creator Science podcast episode](https://podcast.creatorscience.com/george-blackman/)

**Other methods named in the OutlierKit comparison (secondary)**
- MrBeast: word-for-word scripting with retention checkpoints. Tim Schmoyer: hook → promise → payoff. Paddy Galloway: "data-first retention principles", best suited to channels that have their own retention history to learn from. The guide concludes that "No single scriptwriting method wins for every channel" — [OutlierKit](https://outlierkit.com/resources/youtube-scriptwriting-methods-compared/)
- A separate guide gives a five-part structure: hard hook → retention bridge → interrupt loop every 60–90 s → payoff stack → CTA — [Hooked.so](https://www.hooked.so/blog/script-for-youtube-video) (marketing blog; the 60–90 s figure has no data behind it)

**MrBeast production handbook (leaked 2024)**
- Rosanna Pansino posted it in Aug 2024. Passionfruit reports that two former MrBeast producers confirmed it is real; the company declined to comment. Treat it as likely authentic but not officially verified — [Passionfruit](https://passionfru.it/?p=81151); [Net Influencer](https://www.netinfluencer.com/mrbeast-productions-leaked-internal-document-rosanna-pansino/)
- The first minute is the highest-risk stretch. The handbook's example is a video that lost 21M viewers in its first minute out of about 60M initial clicks. The opening's job is to **prove the thumbnail/title promise is being delivered**. Minutes 3–6 are named as the next most important stretch. It also recommends a "wow factor" moment (for example, a house lowered by crane about 30 s into "100 Days in the Circle") — [Passionfruit](https://passionfru.it/?p=81151); [GIGAZINE](https://gigazine.net/gsc_news/en/20240917-mrbeast-youtube-production); [Alexander Jarvis summary](https://www.alexanderjarvis.com/memo-how-to-succeed-in-mrbeast-production/)

**Veritasium: "misconception first"**
- Derek Muller's PhD (University of Sydney) studied how multimedia can address physics misconceptions — [Wikipedia: Derek Muller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derek_Muller)
- Veritasium starts with the most common misconceptions about a topic so the viewer gets "confused" because all of them might be true. It then runs the experiment or explanation and discusses the result afterward — [Big Think](https://bigthink.com/?p=68008)
- The often-cited classroom result: students scored about 6/26 on a gravity pre-test and barely improved after a clear explainer, while the misconception-led video nearly doubled their scores. **Caution:** these numbers come from AI-generated summary sites that disagree on the exact post-test score (11/26 vs "nearly doubled"). Check against Muller's thesis or papers before quoting them on air — [youtubesummary.com](https://youtubesummary.com/summary/QHhJ8_TJeNo); [gist.ly](https://gist.ly/youtube-summarizer/the-secrets-behind-veritasiums-viral-success)
- "Clickbait is Unreasonably Effective" (Aug 17, 2021, **older than 2023**): splits clickbait into an accurate, curiosity-driven type and a misleading type, and argues for "legitbait", meaning titles and thumbnails that entice but represent the video accurately. Muller says title and thumbnail are "at least half" the job. The widely shared line: a platform can't succeed if it is all clickbait, and can't succeed with none either — [Briefy summary](https://briefy.ai/summary/v2/curfkxexuv57xsqmhbo9twba/en/clickbait-is-unreasonably-effective); [GIGAZINE](https://wbgsv0a.gigazine.net/gsc_news/en/20210830-clickbait-effective/) (secondary/AI summaries)

**Kurzgesagt**
- Official process: research from books and primary sources such as papers, then consultation with experts. Scripts aim for "a balance between facts and storytelling" and can take "about a dozen drafts". Every video gets a public **sourcesheet** "to prove our claims and make our process transparent" — [Kurzgesagt: Our video process](https://kurzgesagt.org/youtube/)
- About 1,200 production hours per video (2020 figure, older) — [GIGAZINE](https://www.gigazine.net/gsc_news/en/20200217-science-animation-making/)

### Inferences (templates synthesized from the above)

**Template A — But/Therefore beat sheet (works for any segment)**
1. Status quo: "Here's what everyone assumes…"
2. BUT: "…but [new fact / event] just happened."
3. THEREFORE: "So [consequence for the viewer / market]."
4. BUT: "Except there's a catch: [counter-evidence / risk]."
5. THEREFORE: "Which means the thing to watch is [X]."
Test: read the beats aloud. Wherever you would naturally say "and then", rewrite it or cut it.

**Template B — Setup / Tension / Payoff per segment (Blackman-style)**
- Setup (1–2 sentences): context plus the question this segment answers.
- Tension: the conflict, the surprising number, or the "but".
- Payoff: the answer, plus a one-line "so what for you".
- Bridge: open the next loop before closing out ("…and that matters even more for the Number today").

**Template C — Misconception-first explainer (Veritasium-style)**
1. State the common belief the way a smart viewer would ("Most people think AI agents will replace your accountant this year").
2. Make it plausible and briefly let the viewer agree.
3. Introduce the evidence that breaks it (data, demo, primary source).
4. Explain the real mechanism.
5. Re-state the corrected model in one sentence and say why it matters for the viewer's money or work.
This fits the compliance constraint well, because the structure is about correcting understanding, not recommending trades.

**Template D — MrBeast-handbook-derived opening logic for long videos**
- 0–60 s: deliver the visual or concrete evidence that the title promise is real.
- 1–3 min: build stakes.
- 3–6 min: second-most important stretch, so put a strong mini-payoff or "wow" here.
- After that: re-engage at intervals. The handbook's exact interval guidance could not be verified (see Gaps).

**Template E — Kurzgesagt-style accuracy layer (fits the "stay accurate" constraint)**
- Keep a linked sourcesheet (Google Doc or Notion) for every episode with claim → source → date checked. Link it in the description. This builds trust and matches a not-financial-advice posture.

**Fit with the finance compliance constraint:** but/therefore, misconception-first and setup/tension/payoff are all neutral and fit well. Hook → promise → payoff and "wow factor" thinking risk pushing toward overclaimed promises ("this stock will…"). Use Muller's "legitbait" standard (accurate but enticing) as the guardrail.

### Gaps
- **Paddy Galloway**: no primary source on his script advice was found. Only a secondary label ("data-first retention principles") turned up. His own videos and X threads would be the source.
- **Film Booth (Ed Lawrence) "content–context"**: searches returned nothing relevant, so the framework's exact definition could not be verified. Do not present it as sourced.
- **Johnny Harris / Vox explainer structure**: no primary or credible secondary description was found in this pass. (General knowledge says his work is built around a central question and is map-driven, but this is unverified.)
- **Ali Abdaal's own script framework**: found only indirectly, via Blackman, his former scriptwriter.
- The exact rehook interval ("every 30–90 seconds") has **no primary data behind it** in anything I found. It appears in marketing blogs (Hooked.so says 60–90 s).
- Colin and Samir material on scripting was not reached.

## 2. How finance creators structure episodes and make numbers memorable

### Takeaway
I found little published structural analysis of specific finance channels. The best-documented number-memorability technique is Humphrey Yang's physical-scale visual (rice grains = dollars). Broadcast writing guides add round the number, give it context, and limit how many numbers you use per sentence.

### Cited Findings
- Humphrey Yang counted 10,000 grains of rice to visualize $1B. At $100,000 per grain, Jeff Bezos's wealth came to about 58 lb of rice. Yang said it worked because it was "a shocking visual representation". The two 60-s TikToks drew about 2.2M combined views (2020, **older**) — [Fortune, Mar 2020](https://www.fortune.com/2020/03/21/tik-tok-influencers-personal-finance-advice/)
- A viewer on Yang's newsletter asked for more such videos because they "have a hard time visualizing big numbers… what large amounts of money look like, and what they can buy", which is direct evidence of audience demand for scale anchors — [Hump Days Substack](https://humpdays.substack.com/p/hump-days-slow-and-steady/comments)
- Broadcast guidance: numbers are hard to process by ear, so round them and give context. Avoid jargon and acronyms — [JEA, drawing on NPR's *Sound Reporting* by Jonathan Kern](https://jea.org/digital-media/writing-for-broadcast-how-to-sound-like-a-real-person); [Intro to Journalism (Pressbooks) ch. 8](https://cwi.pressbooks.pub/introductiontojournalismandnewswriting/chapter/chapter-8-writing-for-broadcast-journalism/)

### Inferences
- **Number-memorability toolkit** (my synthesis of the Yang and broadcast principles):
  1. Round on air ("about 4 percent", not "3.87%") and put the precise figure on screen.
  2. **Per-person math**: divide big aggregates by population or households ("$1.2 trillion of credit-card debt works out to roughly $X per household"). Compute it yourself and source the denominator.
  3. **Physical or visual anchor** (Yang): stacks, grains, football fields, or a "days of salary" comparison.
  4. **Familiar-unit comparison**: "That's more than the GDP of [country]" or "that's 3 years of the median rent".
  5. **Change, not level**: "up from X a year ago" is easier to hear than a standalone figure.
  6. One number per sentence. Say the number, then say what it means.
- This maps straight onto the "The Number" segment. Template: *Number (rounded) → what it measures (one plain sentence) → comparison or per-person anchor → why it matters to your money this week → what would change it.* End on a conditional ("if X, watch Y"), never on "so buy/sell".
- Finance-specific compliance: creators who talk about "what I'm doing with my money" (a Graham Stephan / Andrei Jikh-style personal-portfolio framing) sit close to recommendations. The "Money Move" segment is safer framed as process or education ("how to check your expense ratio") than as asset calls.

### Gaps
- No source found describing the episode structure of Graham Stephan, Andrei Jikh, Patrick Boyle, The Plain Bagel, Money & Macro, Ben Felix, How Money Works, CNBC Make It or WSJ explainers. (The search for "How Money Works" mostly returned a DK book of the same name.) A transcript analysis of 3–5 videos per channel would be needed.
- No published study on which number-framing technique gives the best recall on YouTube specifically.

## 3. Daily news formats: sequencing multiple stories, transitions, segment count and length

### Takeaway
The two models are (a) a **multi-story brief**: NPR's *Up First* is about 10 minutes (originally the first 10 min of *Morning Edition*) with scripted segments and a podcast-specific top and tail that previews the episode; and (b) a **single-story narrative**: WSJ's *The Journal* tells one story per day in 15–20 min with a classic story arc. *Morning Brew Daily* is a roughly 30-min conversational show with recurring weekly features. DeFranco found that splitting into many separate segment-shows confused his audience.

### Cited Findings
- *Up First* began as the first ~10 minutes of *Morning Edition*, edited into a podcast by 6 a.m., with a "more podcast-y tail and top", including a preview of the day's episode (2017, **older**) — [Nieman Lab, Apr 2017](https://www.niemanlab.org/2017/04/nprs-morning-edition-gets-a-little-refresh-and-its-own-new-podcast/)
- *Up First* scripts are drafted by producers and reviewed before recording. Hosts go over scripts together, and producers, an editor and a director re-record intros and fix mispronunciations (2018, **older**) — [KERA News](https://www.keranews.org/post/intimate-look-how-first-nprs-news-podcast-made-fresh-every-day)
- NPR expanded *Up First* to Saturdays in 2019, so the daily brief now has a weekend edition — [Current, Nov 2019](https://current.org/2019/11/npr-expands-up-first-to-saturdays/)
- *The Journal* (WSJ/Spotify) tells "one important news story in 15–20 minutes" each day. Producers script and shape narratives with attention to "pacing, tone, and story arc" over multiple rounds of edits — [Wikipedia: The Journal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Journal_(podcast)); [Sounds Profitable job listing](https://soundsprofitable.com/jobs/producer-the-journal/)
- *Morning Brew Daily*: a daily talk show of about 30 minutes covering "the most noteworthy news of the day". It adds research, facts and figures, analysis and context beyond the newsletter, and has recurring features such as weekly "Stocks of the Week" and an end-of-week "what you need to know in the week ahead" — [Pocket Casts blog, Aug 2025](https://blog.pocketcasts.com/2025/08/22/freyman-and-howell/); [Pod Engine listing](https://www.podengine.ai/podcasts/morning-brew-daily)
- Philip DeFranco: testing several separate daily segments **confused his audience**, which led to spinning them out into SourceFed. In 2018 he split controversial single-story breakdowns (morning) from the full afternoon show — [Wikipedia: SourceFed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SourceFed); [Tubefilter, Aug 2018](https://tubefilter.com/2018/08/20/philip-defranco-show-new-format/) (**older**)
- Broadcast rule: lead with what is current and condense, since a print story may need to fit into about 30 seconds on air — [Intro to Journalism (Pressbooks) ch. 8](https://cwi.pressbooks.pub/introductiontojournalismandnewswriting/chapter/chapter-8-writing-for-broadcast-journalism/)

### Inferences
- **Avoiding the "list of headlines" feel**: the evidence points to one lead story with real depth (the *Journal* model), plus quick items whose transitions are causal (but/therefore), not "next up". A transition sentence should name the **link** between stories ("That same chip shortage is why today's Number is…"). When there is no real link, use a hard, branded segment bumper instead of a fake segue. Honest separation beats a forced link.
- **Proposed daily 6–10 min brief (~1,000–1,500 words at 150–165 WPM)**, mapped to the existing segments:
  | Segment | Target length | Script job |
  |---|---|---|
  | Cold open | 10–20 s | The single most surprising fact or question of the day; proves the title promise (MrBeast-handbook logic) |
  | Intro line | 5 s | Fixed catchphrase plus date; preview of 2–3 items (the *Up First* "top") |
  | AI Move | 90–120 s | Lead story; but/therefore arc |
  | Both sides | 60–90 s | Steelman each side. Fits a no-advice posture and builds trust |
  | The Number | 45–60 s | Number template from Q2 |
  | Quick hits | 60–90 s, 3 items max | ~20–25 s each; one sentence of "so what" |
  | Chart of the day | 30–45 s | One visual anchor, one takeaway |
  | Money Move | 45–60 s | Process or education only, no buy/sell |
  | On the calendar | 20–30 s | Builds a reason to come back tomorrow (open loop) |
  | Disclaimer | 5–8 s | Fixed wording; can sit in the intro or close and also on screen and in the description |
  | Close + comment question | 10–15 s | End fast (see Q6) |
  That is 9–11 segments, which is a lot for 6–10 minutes. Either keep each one tight or rotate some (for example Chart of the day on alternate days). DeFranco's experience suggests keeping a **consistent, predictable** spine rather than constantly reshuffling segments.
- The "Both sides" segment is the strongest compliance asset in the format. Present it as education, not a verdict.

### Gaps
- No source gave a researched optimal segment count for YouTube daily news. TLDR News, Bloomberg/Reuters short explainers and DeFranco's sponsor placement were not documented in the sources reached.
- Exact transition-writing conventions (fully scripted vs host-ad-libbed) at *Up First* and *The Journal* are not publicly documented.

## 4. Writing for the ear: sentence length, WPM, contractions, "you" language, scripting vs ad-lib

### Takeaway
Broadcast guides agree: write the way you talk, short sentences, active voice with the actor first, no jargon, round numbers, read it aloud. Conversational speech runs about 150 WPM, and simplified video narration measured at about 169 WPM (range 128–203). No transcript-based WPM data for specific top YouTubers was found.

### Cited Findings
- NPR (Kern, *Sound Reporting*): "write the way you speak". Picture a single listener such as your mom. Use short sentences, since they're easier to follow by ear and give natural pauses — [JEA](https://jea.org/digital-media/writing-for-broadcast-how-to-sound-like-a-real-person)
- Put the actor first and use active voice. Read the script aloud, because a sentence that trips you up while reading will trip you up on air. Cut jargon and acronyms. Round numbers and give context. Lead with what's current — [Intro to Journalism (Pressbooks)](https://cwi.pressbooks.pub/introductiontojournalismandnewswriting/chapter/chapter-8-writing-for-broadcast-journalism/); [Human Kinetics excerpt](https://us.humankinetics.com/blogs/excerpt/tips-for-writing-for-broadcast); [CBI "Writing for the Ear" (2019)](https://www.askcbi.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/11/Writing-for-the-Ear-NSEMC-2019-FINAL-1.pdf)
- WPM benchmarks: conversation is ~120–150 WPM. Biteable's analysis of 100 of its own videos averaged 169 WPM (range 128–203); simple language allows a faster pace. Simon Sinek is cited at ~175 and Tony Robbins at ~200 — [Biteable](https://biteable.com/blog/video-word-count/)
- US English conversational rate is ~150 WPM (National Center for Voice and Speech); radio hosts and podcasters run 150–160 WPM — [VirtualSpeech](https://virtualspeech.com/blog/average-speaking-rate-words-per-)
- Planning heuristic: a 10-min video needs about 1,200–1,800 words depending on pace (third-party tool, not measured creator data) — [touhfa.art script timer](https://touhfa.art/tools/youtube-script-length-calculator/)
- MrBeast's team uses word-for-word scripting with retention checkpoints (secondary) — [OutlierKit](https://outlierkit.com/resources/youtube-scriptwriting-methods-compared/)
- NPR's *Up First* is scripted, and hosts re-record intros and fix mispronunciations, which shows professional "natural" delivery is usually scripted and then polished — [KERA](https://www.keranews.org/post/intimate-look-how-first-nprs-news-podcast-made-fresh-every-day)

### Inferences
- **Targets for this creator**: script the daily brief at ~155–165 WPM, so 8 min ≈ 1,250–1,300 words. Weekend 30-min videos: ~4,000–4,500 words of script, with demos taking up the remaining time.
- **Ear-writing checklist**: ≤ ~15–20 words per sentence on average, with a mix of short punches; contractions on; "you/your" in every segment's "so what"; one idea per sentence; one number per sentence; no unexplained acronyms (say "large language model" once before "LLM"); subject-verb-object order; put the key word at the end of the sentence for emphasis.
- **Scripting vs bullets (conflict)**: news and finance formats need word-for-word lines wherever accuracy or compliance matters: numbers, claims, the disclaimer, "both sides" wording. Bullets or ad-lib suit reactions and personality beats. A hybrid (scripted facts, bulleted color) settles it. The evidence favors scripting for accuracy (NPR practice); the claim that ad-lib reads as more "natural" comes from creator anecdote only.
- To make a read script sound natural: write in spoken rhythm, mark emphasis and breath pauses, rehearse once aloud, and allow small paraphrases on non-factual lines.

### Gaps
- No measured WPM for Ali Abdaal, MrBeast, Graham Stephan or other named creators was found. A transcript-word-count / runtime analysis would fill this.
- The NPR Training website itself was not reached. Its guidance here comes via secondary sources citing Kern's book.

## 5. Structuring a 30-minute tutorial/workflow video and a 30-minute weekly recap

### Takeaway
For tutorials, sources agree: **show the finished result first**, state who it's for and a concrete promise, keep theory short and put it after the steps, and use chapters. For long videos generally, the MrBeast handbook marks the first minute and minutes 3–6 as critical. I found no sourced, tested structure for a weekly news recap.

### Cited Findings
- Show a short clip of the finished output before any hook or context, since search viewers are goal-driven. Then state who the video is for and make a specific promise. Put context and theory **after** the steps; front-loading theory hurts retention — [TubeMilestone](https://tubemilestone.com/blog/structure-youtube-tutorial)
- A decision-based flow: setup → method → demonstration → troubleshooting → final check, each section answering a distinct question — [Subscribr](https://subscribr.ai/youtube-strategy/structure-youtube-videos-watch-time-seo). Similar order: promised result → prerequisites → setup → execution → troubleshooting → recap — [Timeskip](https://timeskip.io/blog/good-video-ideas)
- Long tutorials (freeCodeCamp's multi-hour videos) work because they are "meticulously structured with clear sections and timestamps" — [Subscribr](https://subscribr.ai/youtube-strategy/structure-youtube-videos-watch-time-seo)
- Anecdote: cutting a 45-s intro to 3 s raised 30-s retention from 52% to 78% (single, unverified anecdote) — [ytshark](https://ytshark.com/how-to-increase-watch-time-on-youtube-7-proven-strategies/)
- MrBeast handbook: the first minute must prove the promise, and minutes 3–6 are the next most important stretch — [Passionfruit](https://passionfru.it/?p=81151)
- *Morning Brew Daily* ends some weekly episodes with "what you need to know in the week ahead", a forward-looking close for a recap — [Pod Engine listing](https://www.podengine.ai/podcasts/morning-brew-daily)
- *The Journal* model: one important story told with a full story arc — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Journal_(podcast))

### Inferences
**Template — 30-min "AI Money Workflow" tutorial**
1. 0:00–0:30 — End result on screen ("By the end you'll have a spreadsheet that categorizes your bank export with AI in 2 minutes"). Who it's for, what you need.
2. 0:30–1:30 — Stakes and the misconception ("Most people think you need to code for this…"). Chapter preview.
3. Chapters 1–N (about 4–6 min each): each one opens with its mini-goal, then do → show result (checkpoint: "if your screen looks like this, you're on track") → common error and fix → one-line bridge to the next chapter's problem (but/therefore).
4. Around minute 3–6: the first big "wow" mini-payoff (first automation runs).
5. Last chapter: limitations, privacy, accuracy checks. This is where the compliance and responsible-AI caveats go ("check the AI's math; this isn't tax advice").
6. Close: recap in 3 bullets, point to the specific next video, end.
Add YouTube chapters/timestamps matching the script headers.

**Template — 30-min "The Week in AI and Money" recap**
1. Cold open: the one story that mattered most, as a question.
2. Preview: "Five stories, ranked by how much they affect your money."
3. Countdown #5 → #2 (~3–4 min each), each with but/therefore and a "so what for you". Insert a "Number of the week" and "Chart of the week" between them as rehooks.
4. #1: "The one story that mattered" (~8–10 min, *Journal*-style mini-narrative: what happened → why → both sides → what to watch).
5. Viewer comments from the week's daily episodes (community and callbacks).
6. "The week ahead" calendar (forward loop, Morning Brew model).
7. Disclaimer + comment question + end.
The countdown creates a built-in open loop (the viewer waits for #1). The trade-off is that viewers who came for #1 may skip ahead. Chapters make that easy, so judge it by total watch time, not only the retention curve.

### Gaps
- No primary data on optimal chapter length for 30-min educational videos.
- No sourced study on countdown vs chronological recap formats.

## 6. CTAs and endings

### Takeaway
End screens are officially limited to the last 5–20 s, with up to 4 elements. Creator-blog consensus: don't signal "wrapping up" early, fold the CTA into the content, keep talking over the end screen, give one recommended video rather than four, and end soon after the last piece of value. All of the strategy advice beyond the official mechanics is opinion from third-party blogs.

### Cited Findings
- Official: end screens go in the **last 5–20 seconds**. The video must be at least 25 s long, with up to 4 elements on 16:9. Cards and watermarks are hidden during the end screen. Not available on made-for-kids videos. Best practices: leave room in the edit, stagger elements, and track performance in the Engagement tab — [YouTube Help: Add end screens](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/6388789)
- "Premature exit": retention drops sharply in the last 10–20% when creators say "to wrap up" or switch from value to asking for likes and subscribes. Advice: never signal the ending until it actually comes, and build the CTA into the content rather than tacking it on — [Subscribr](https://subscribr.ai/youtube-strategy/youtube-analytics-fix-viewer-drop-off)
- Keep the creator visible and talking during the end screen, because going silent and fading out tells viewers the video is over. One recommended video plus subscribe beats four options "in most cases" (blog claim, no data shown) — [Faceless.so](https://faceless.so/blog/9-youtube-end-screen-strategies-for-more-subscribers-1)
- A sharp drop in the final 20 s means few people see the end screen, which is a pacing problem, not a design one — [TubeMilestone](https://tubemilestone.com/blog/youtube-end-screen-ctr)
- Conflict: one guide says some end drop-off is normal, while a very sharp one suggests the outro is too long or the CTA poorly integrated — [Tubecore](https://tubecore.xpanddigital.io/blog/youtube-audience-retention-guide)
- A "healthy" end-screen CTR of 3–7% is a blog figure, not an official benchmark — [TubeMilestone](https://tubemilestone.com/blog/youtube-end-screen-ctr)
- Once the final bit of value is delivered, end the video — [SocialVideoPlaza](https://www.socialvideoplaza.com/en/articles/length-youtube-outro)

### Inferences
- **Daily brief close**: put the comment question **inside** the last content beat ("On the calendar: Nvidia reports Wednesday. Here's my question for you: [question]"), then disclaimer and sign-off in under 15 s over the end screen, with **one** element (tomorrow's or yesterday's episode playlist) plus subscribe.
- **Subscribe ask placement**: put a short, value-tied ask after the first payoff (for example after the AI Move: "if this saved you a search, that's what the daily brief does"), not in the first 15 seconds. This follows from the "prove the promise first" logic; there's no direct A/B evidence for it.
- **Disclaimer placement**: say a short version early (after the intro line), since viewers who leave early still heard it, and repeat it on screen and in the description. A long spoken disclaimer at the end eats the close. Ask the creator's compliance or legal advisor which placement they prefer.
- The "end abruptly" advice works with a no-advice posture as long as the disclaimer isn't the thing that gets cut.

### Gaps
- No YouTube-official data or controlled study comparing mid-roll vs end subscribe asks.

## 7. Keeping a daily series fresh

### Takeaway
I found little direct evidence. The relevant signals are recurring branded features (Morning Brew's "Stocks of the Week" and "week ahead"), a fixed spine (DeFranco's segment experiment confused his audience), and scripted top/tail packaging (*Up First*).

### Cited Findings
- Morning Brew Daily uses recurring weekly features ("Stocks of the Week") and guest co-hosts for variety — [Pod Engine](https://www.podengine.ai/podcasts/morning-brew-daily); [Pocket Casts](https://blog.pocketcasts.com/2025/08/22/freyman-and-howell/)
- DeFranco's multi-segment test confused viewers, so a predictable format matters — [Wikipedia: SourceFed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SourceFed)
- *Up First* added a podcast-specific top and tail with a preview — [Nieman Lab](https://www.niemanlab.org/2017/04/nprs-morning-edition-gets-a-little-refresh-and-its-own-new-podcast/)

### Inferences
- **Freshness levers that keep the spine fixed**: (1) a callback line ("Yesterday I said watch the CPI print. Here's what happened"), which closes open loops from "On the calendar" and rewards daily viewers; (2) read one viewer comment on air from the prior episode's comment question, which closes the loop on the CTA and gives a reason to comment; (3) weekly rotating mini-features (a "Friday AI tool test", say) inside the Money Move slot; (4) running tallies ("prediction scorecard"), framed carefully so they aren't trade calls; (5) a fixed catchphrase intro and outro for brand recognition.
- Feed daily open loops into the weekend recap ("the story that mattered") so the three formats reinforce each other.

### Gaps
- No sourced data on callback or comment-on-air effects on retention or comment rate.
- TLDR News and Bloomberg/Reuters short-explainer practices were not documented in the sources reached.
